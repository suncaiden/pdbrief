Last week's issue covered a French trial in which a diabetes drug appeared to hold Parkinson's symptoms steady for a year. It ended by noting that a larger trial of a related drug was due, and that its result would matter a great deal.

That result is in. It is negative.

:::key
In a {{phase 3 trial}} of nearly 200 people followed for almost two years, weekly exenatide made no difference to Parkinson's symptoms compared with placebo. The trial was well designed and adequately sized. The most likely explanation is the simplest one: the drug does not work for this.
:::

## The hope that led here

Exenatide had one of the better stories in Parkinson's research.

A {{phase 2 trial}} published in 2017 followed about 60 people for 48 weeks and found that those on exenatide were, on average, about 4 points better on the motor scale than those on placebo — and, importantly, the gap persisted 12 weeks after everyone stopped the drug. For a field that had watched candidate after candidate fail, this was a real result, and it drove a decade of follow-on work.

It was still a small trial. That is what {{phase 2 trial|phase 2}} means: a signal worth chasing, not a conclusion. Chasing it properly is exactly what this new trial did.

## What they did

The trial enrolled around 194 people with Parkinson's across multiple UK centres. Participants were randomly assigned to a weekly injection of 2 mg exenatide or a matching placebo, {{blinding|double-blind}}, and followed for 96 weeks — close to two years, roughly twice the length of the earlier trial.

The {{primary endpoint}} was the motor examination score on the {{MDS-UPDRS}}, measured in the practically-defined off state: participants withheld their usual Parkinson's medication overnight before assessment. This is a deliberately demanding choice. It strips out the effect of levodopa and tries to reveal the underlying state of the disease.

## What they found

No difference. The exenatide group and the placebo group progressed at essentially the same rate over 96 weeks.

The absence of an effect was consistent across the secondary measures too — {{non-motor symptoms}}, quality of life, medication requirements, and imaging. There was no subgroup that clearly benefited. There was no hint of a signal that a longer or larger trial might have sharpened.

This is, in its way, a clean result. Ambiguous negative trials leave the field arguing for years. This one does not.

## Why it matters

**A decade-long hypothesis has been tested properly and did not hold.** That is how the process is meant to work, and it is worth saying plainly: a negative trial from a well-run study is a real contribution. It stops other groups spending years and money on the same idea, and it stops patients enrolling in studies of something that does not help.

**It shows what phase 2 results are worth.** The 2017 trial was not fraudulent or incompetent. It was small, and small trials produce unstable estimates. Chance, a strong {{placebo}} response, and the enthusiasm that surrounds an early positive result can combine to produce a promising number that evaporates under scrutiny. This happens constantly, in every area of medicine.

**It complicates last week's story rather than ending it.** The lixisenatide trial was positive; this exenatide trial was negative. They are different drugs, different doses, different endpoints — lixisenatide was assessed on medication, exenatide off it — and different populations. Reconciling them is now an open problem.

:::caution
If you or someone you care for is taking a GLP-1 drug for diabetes or weight management, this changes nothing about that. The drugs work for what they were designed to do. This trial asked a different question — whether exenatide slows Parkinson's — and the answer was no.
:::

## Reading the two trials together

| | Lixisenatide (France, 2024) | Exenatide (UK, 2025) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Participants | about 156 | about 194 |
| Duration | 12 months | 96 weeks |
| Assessed | on medication | off medication |
| Result | roughly 3-point benefit | no difference |

Several explanations are live, and the honest position is that nobody yet knows which is right.

It may be that assessing people on their usual medication, as the French trial did, is more forgiving — capturing a modest symptomatic effect rather than true disease modification. It may be that 12 months is short enough for a small early difference to appear and 96 weeks is long enough for it to wash out. It may be that the two drugs genuinely differ in how much reaches the brain. Or the French result may simply have been a false positive, of the same kind the 2017 exenatide trial now appears to have been.

The next few years of follow-up work will sort this out. Until then, the field's overall position on GLP-1 drugs for Parkinson's has moved from cautiously hopeful to distinctly doubtful.

## What to watch next

Whether the lixisenatide finding replicates in a larger trial. That is now the decisive question for this whole line of research.

Whether attention shifts. Groups working on {{alpha-synuclein}}-targeting antibodies, {{LRRK2}} inhibitors, and {{GBA1}}-directed therapies have their own late-stage trials running. Funding and enthusiasm tend to follow results.

And whether trial design changes. Both of these trials enrolled people who already had a diagnosis. The argument that treatments must start earlier — in the {{prodromal}} stage, identified by tests like the {{seed amplification assay}} we covered two weeks ago — gets stronger every time a trial in diagnosed patients comes back empty.

:::plain
A big, careful trial of exenatide found it does no good in Parkinson's, contradicting a smaller trial from 2017 that had raised hopes. Nothing about your treatment changes. What changes is which ideas the research world spends the next few years pursuing — and this is how that gets decided.
:::
